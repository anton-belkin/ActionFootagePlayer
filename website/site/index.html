The full range

Turn off your camera's compatibility mode. Capture the scene in all its beauty.

Your camera aims for the average screen

Most cameras are built for standard screens. By default, every clip is processed to look good right away on an average phone or office screen. Modern cameras squeeze a scene's range cleverly, but they have to make trade-offs, and the clip can no longer use the extra brightness and color that most phones and TVs can show (that is what HDR means). In effect, the default mode is a compatibility mode.

The full range, now for everyone

Most of these cameras can also keep the full range, but it hides behind a pro setting called log, and getting a natural look from it on those screens used to take professional software. Action Footage Player does it as you press play, so a sunset, a sunny day, a dive or any high-contrast scene looks the way you remember it.

The trade-off, plainly

Most apps show log clips flat and grey, so you watch them in Action Footage Player or export them first. And on standard screens, even through it, log looks a little worse than the camera's default mode. You give up a little convenience and some sharing quality to keep the moment in full. A scene-aware conversion for standard screens is on the roadmap, and it will apply to the log you have already shot.

How it works

Three steps, and the second one is a double-click.

  1. Shoot in log

    If you watch on HDR screens, set the camera to D-Log M (or GP-Log, I-Log). Under water, also turn EIS off: only then does the camera record the motion data.

  2. Double-click a clip

    It opens in HDR, stabilized, with the telemetry the camera recorded. Highlights you marked with the camera's button show up on the seek bar.

  3. Share a moment

    Press l twice to mark a part, then Shift+E. You get an HDR file that iPhones, Macs and YouTube show correctly.

What it does

Everything happens while the clip plays.

Log in true HDR

D-Log M, D-Log, GP-Log, GP-Log2 and 10-bit I-Log are converted with curves the color community measured on the cameras themselves, or with the makers' own formulas where they publish them, and shown as HDR, with no look added: the scene's brightness relationships stay as they were. Made for HDR screens: that is where log beats the camera's default mode.

D-Log M · D-Log · GP-Log · GP-Log2 · I-Log (10-bit)

Stabilized during playback

Built on Gyroflow's engine and the motion data your camera already writes into every clip. Nothing is rendered before you can watch. Three modes: stabilization with crop, stabilization with filled edges, or off.

gyro + accelerometer from the file

Telemetry on screen

Depth, whether the camera is in the water, GPS and speed appear when the camera recorded them; altitude, pressure and exposure are there in the menu. Fields the clip doesn't have stay hidden.

depth · GPS · speed · ISO · shutter

One-key export

The part you're watching is exported as you see it, stabilization included, in one pass. You get a natural HDR file to share or to adjust to your taste. Exports slow down while you play and run at full speed when you pause.

Under water

Steady footage below the surface.

You already know in-camera stabilization falls apart under water. Turn EIS off, keep shooting, and let Action Footage Player stabilize from the motion data, with a correction for how water bends light through the lens.

Tuned for water by default

The default preset accounts for refraction at the camera's flat lens cover. On the Osmo Action 6 it follows the camera's own in-water flag, so a clip that goes in and out of the water is corrected only where it's in. Above water, DJI's own EIS works well; for EIS-off clips shot in air, Ctrl+u takes the lens out of the water.

Depth where the camera records it

The Osmo Action 6 logs depth and water status (in a dive housing, only the water status). Turn the readout on and off with Ctrl+t.

No color fringes from the water

Water bends blue more than red, so red and blue land apart towards the frame's edges. Wherever the lens is under water, each color is put back where it belongs. Ctrl+a leaves the fringes as recorded.

Questions

Before you download.

Do I need an HDR screen?

Yes, if you want the benefit. Log pays off when you, and most of the people you share with, watch on HDR screens: recent iPhones, MacBook Pros, HDR TVs and monitors.

On a standard screen, a log clip looks worse than the same scene recorded in the camera's default mode. That is true whether macOS tone-maps it automatically or, for D-Log M, you switch to DJI's official SDR conversion (its Rec.709 LUT) with Ctrl+s. The camera makers' own processing adapts to each scene and keeps bright what most likely matters in it. Getting the same result from log takes professional grading software and a colorist.

So if you mostly watch and share on standard screens, record in the default mode. If you and your audience have HDR screens, record in log.

For clips you mainly share to standard screens, you can still use the camera maker's app. A scene-aware SDR conversion in Action Footage Player is on the roadmap: squeezing the range always takes decisions, and the aim is to keep the result as close as possible to the full-range version rather than add a look of its own. When it lands, the log footage you have already shot gets better too.

I dive and snorkel. Do I need this?

Yes. Under water, log played in HDR is clearly better than the camera's default mode, especially on recent cameras. The difference is night and day. A dive scene runs from bright light at the surface to dark blue at depth, and the default mode has to squeeze all of it into a standard screen, with trade-offs along the way.

On top of that, you get stabilization that works under water (turn EIS off when you shoot) and the depth readout, if your camera records it.

Who needs the stabilization in the player?

If you shoot above water with the camera's stabilization (EIS) on, you probably don't: in-camera EIS does a good job in air.

It's for clips shot with EIS off. Under water, where in-camera EIS fails, that is the way to shoot. It also covers clips where you turned EIS off for the widest view, and archives full of such footage. The camera still records its motion data, and Action Footage Player uses it while the clip plays, with a choice of stabilization with crop, stabilization with filled edges, or off.

One exception: on GoPro HERO8 and newer, you can leave HyperSmooth on under water. Its result won't be perfect, and the player stabilizes it further from the same motion data. On HERO5, HERO6 and HERO7 Black, HyperSmooth has to be off. (This follows Gyroflow's camera notes; GoPro footage in the player is still waiting to be verified.)

Which computers does it run on?

Mac: Apple Silicon with macOS 15 or later. Windows: 10 or 11 on 64-bit Intel or AMD. The Mac app is the one in daily use; the Windows app carries the same plugins but hasn't been tried on a real PC yet.

Neither app is signed by a developer account yet. On a Mac, allow it once in System Settings, Privacy & Security, Open Anyway. On Windows, SmartScreen shows a warning on first launch: More info, Run anyway.

Does it change my original files?

No. Action Footage Player only reads them. Exports are written to a stabilized/ folder next to the clip, and a clip you open in Gyroflow gets its project as a .gyroflow file beside it; the video file itself is never written to.

What does an exported file look like?

An HEVC Main10 file tagged as HLG, which iPhones, Macs, most HDR TVs and YouTube display as HDR. If you edit afterwards, the command-line export can write ProRes 422 HQ instead.

The file has no look added, so it is a natural starting point: adjust it to your taste in an HDR-capable editor such as iMovie. A look that is baked into a file can't be taken out again: shadows pushed to black stay black.

Why not just use the camera maker's app?

Use it if you like its look. Phone apps are great for quick edits, and some can now show and export log as HDR. That HDR tends to be styled for social media: exposure that shifts as the scene changes, brighter mid-tones, strong local contrast, deep shadows pushed to black.

Action Footage Player does the opposite: a direct conversion from log that keeps the scene's brightness relationships and adds no look of its own, so the clip looks the way the scene did. You can always add a look later; you can't take one out.

It also runs on your computer, plays your whole archive as it is, works with footage from several camera makers, and stabilizes while it plays.

Is it really free?

Yes. It's open source under GPL-3.0, and the code is on GitHub.

Nothing is locked, and there is no account to make.
