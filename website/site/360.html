360 video

Look around inside a 360° clip, as it plays.

Open a clip from a 360 camera and drag, or move two fingers on the trackpad: you look around the scene while it plays. The horizon stays level, and the view turns as fast as your screen can show it.

How it works

The sphere comes from the camera. Your view is turned on the screen.

A 360 camera records two very wide lenses, back to back. The player makes one sphere of them as the clip plays, then shows you the part you're looking at.

One sphere from two lenses

For a clip straight from an Insta360 X5, Gyroflow's engine, the same that steadies the player's other clips, stitches both lenses into one sphere. It knows each lens from the file, and it takes out the wobble of the camera's rolling shutter.

The horizon held

The camera's gyroscope says how it was tilted in every frame. The player turns the sphere back by as much, so the horizon stays level wherever you look, even when the camera leaned.

Your view, at the screen's rate

Turning your view doesn't go through the stabilization. It's done on the graphics card just before each picture is shown. On a Mac with a 120 Hz screen, a turn reaches the screen in about 9 ms in a window: it feels attached to your fingers.

Already stitched clips too

A 360 video exported from the camera maker's app, with the usual 360 metadata, is built to open the same way. It has no motion data any more, so it plays as it was stitched, and only your view turns.

Looking around

Drag, pinch, and look ahead again.

The view turns on by itself when a 360 clip opens, playing or paused. The side panel opens on its 360° view part.

Projections

From a normal view to a little planet.

How the sphere is laid flat on your screen. Projection in the panel, or Alt+p.

Auto

Straight lines stay straight up to a 100° view, like a normal camera; zoom out further and more and more of the sphere shows, all of it from 160°. The default.

Rectilinear

Straight lines straight, as through a camera's lens. Stretched toward the edges in a wide view.

Stereographic

Shapes kept right to the edges, up to nearly the whole sphere at once.

Little planet

Looking straight down, with the way ahead at the top: the world as a small round planet. Drag around the middle to spin it.

Straight ahead

Ahead along the camera, or held in the world.

When you let go, the view keeps facing the same way. Follows in the panel, or Alt+h, says which way that is. Modes the clip can't give are greyed, with the reason.

Camera

Where the camera points, smoothed over a few seconds, so a mounted camera's turns don't jerk the view. The default for a clip without GPS.

World

Where the camera looked at the start, held. Without a compass or GPS to hold it, it slowly drifts with the gyroscope.

Way

Along the way you're moving, from the GPS track. It needs a clip recorded with GPS: on an Insta360, with its GPS remote or the phone app's GPS on.

North

True north, from the GPS track. Insta360 cameras have no compass, so it needs GPS there too.

Camera and World are tried on a real clip. Way and North are built and tested on made-up motion, not yet on a real clip with GPS: a short one from you would help.

Cameras

Which 360 cameras work.

Exporting

A level 360 video, or a normal one along your view. Coming soon

The export engine can already write both: the whole sphere as a 360 video with the horizon held, for YouTube or a headset, or a normal 16:9 video that follows where you looked. Saving them from the player with a key, as other clips, is next.

Limits

Good to know.

  • Tried on few clips so far. Clips from your camera are the fastest way to make it work well with it.
  • The horizon in hard turns. When the camera leans far in a fast curve, the horizon can be off by about 10°.
  • Opening an .insv file: drag it onto the player's window. Double-clicking it in FinderFile Explorer isn't set up yet.
  • The first second. Right after a clip opens, while the motion data loads, a few frames can be skipped.
  • Clips over 30 fps play every other frame: an 8K camera at 60 fps is more than a sphere can keep up with as it plays.
  • Full screen turns a little slower than a window (about 30 ms instead of 9), because macOS then waits for the screen's refresh.
