About

Made by a diver, for his own footage first.

I'm Anton Belkin. I'm in my forties and live in Geneva with my wife and our two children. I've been traveling for twenty years and diving for fifteen, always with one camera in one hand. This player is what I wanted for my own footage, and now it's free for everyone.

Beauty as it was.

I love travel and diving for the beauty you get to see, and I love sharing it. What I don't love is spending evenings grading and polishing clips. I don't think beauty needs improving: it's already there. Technology should just carry it to the screen as it was.

Better cameras, harder files.

Over the years cameras got better, and their files stopped being ordinary videos. Use everything a camera can do, and you get a grey log picture, gyro data, telemetry and highlight marks that ordinary players can't read. Turn it all off, and the video is plainer than the camera could have made it.

In between there's only the maker's app: on a phone, like watching through a keyhole. You're tied to it for good, often with an account, a cloud or a subscription you never asked for.

Shaky under water.

Under water, everything shakes. When cameras got their own stabilization I was thrilled, until I saw it doesn't work there. And I never wanted a big two-handed rig. For me the dive comes first: being there, looking around, feeling the place. The camera is one hand, no more, or the joy goes.

The divers I talked to were sure of it: digital stabilization doesn't work under water, and can't. I studied physics, so I looked for the reason. A camera's stabilization from its gyroscope assumes how the lens bends light, and water changes that: the lens behaves like a longer one, by about a third. So every turn of the camera moves the picture about a third more than the stabilization expects, and what it misses shows as shake. Correct the lens for water's refraction, and the shake goes.

For a while I used a dome port: a housing with a dome-shaped window over the lens. Light then crosses from water to air straight on, so it isn't bent, and even the camera's own stabilization works. But the dome is plastic and scratches easily, it's big, and the air inside it pulls you toward the surface. It worked as a stop-gap. It wasn't what I wanted.

In April 2024 I took the math to Gyroflow, the open-source stabilization project. With its developers, we worked it out in a few days, and Gyroflow got an underwater setting: tick Lens is underwater, and dives come out steady. The same day I asked GoPro for it in GoPro Labs, their experimental features. About half a year later, Labs got Dive Mode, which adapts HyperSmooth to water's refraction. I've used it ever since, and it works. It hasn't made it into the cameras' regular settings, though.

Then my wife gave me an Osmo Action 6. Its large sensor catches far more light under water, but its stabilization has the same problem. In May 2026 I asked DJI for the same fix. Until it comes, this player does it.

A lucky mix, I think: physics, technology, and a love of diving.

A player that does it all.

I follow technology closely, AI most of all. At some point I saw that it had become good enough for someone with my limited programming knowledge to build a real app. So I built the player I wanted, with Claude, Anthropic's AI, as my programming partner. Double-click a clip, and it plays right away: log in color and in HDR, steady, the lens corrected for water, the color fringes at the edges taken out. Nothing to render, nothing to import.

I made it for myself. Then I saw how many people have the same footage and the same problem. So I made it work for more cameras, tested it on my old GoPro clips, and opened it up. Along the way I've also been giving improvements back to Gyroflow itself.

Open source, and what's next.

This is my first open-source project, and I'm curious myself where it goes. It's free, and it stays free. It does take my time, though. If you find it useful and want to support it, I'd be glad and grateful: support means more time for it, and for more cameras.
